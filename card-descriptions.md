# New Deal Card Game — Historical Descriptions

All descriptions target a high school junior reading level. Each card gets a 2-sentence description explaining what it is/was and why it mattered during the New Deal era (1932–1937).

---

## People

**Franklin Delano Roosevelt** (1932)
Franklin Delano Roosevelt was the 32nd President of the United States, elected in a landslide over Herbert Hoover in 1932 during the worst of the Great Depression, when roughly 25 percent of the workforce was unemployed. He launched the New Deal — a sweeping series of federal programs focused on relief for the unemployed, recovery of the economy, and reform of the financial system — beginning with a historic first 100 days that produced landmark legislation like the Emergency Banking Act, the creation of the FDIC, and the Civilian Conservation Corps.

**Louis McHenry Howe** (1933)
Louis McHenry Howe was a former newspaper reporter who became FDR's most devoted political advisor starting in 1909, managing his campaigns and keeping his political career alive after Roosevelt was partially paralyzed by polio in 1921. Serving as Secretary to the President (a role equivalent to today's White House Chief of Staff), Howe helped shape early New Deal programs — particularly the Civilian Conservation Corps — and mentored Eleanor Roosevelt into public life before his death in 1936.

**Brain Trust** (1932)
The Brain Trust was a group of Columbia University professors — originally Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell, and Adolf Berle — recruited to advise FDR during his 1932 presidential campaign, a term coined by New York Times reporter James Kieran. They helped design the major policies of the First New Deal, including the National Recovery Administration and the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, and their influence represented an unprecedented role for academic experts in shaping presidential policy.

**Raymond Moley** (1932)
Raymond Moley was a Columbia University political economist who organized and led FDR's original Brain Trust, recruiting fellow professors Tugwell and Berle to advise Roosevelt during the 1932 campaign. He wrote most of FDR's first inaugural address and was a powerful early architect of the New Deal, but he began breaking with Roosevelt by mid-1933 over policy disagreements and eventually became a prominent conservative critic of the administration.

**Rexford Guy Tugwell** (1933)
Rexford Guy Tugwell was a Columbia University economist and Brain Trust member who believed the federal government needed large-scale economic planning to pull the country out of the Depression because private business was too paralyzed to act. As Assistant and then Under Secretary of Agriculture, he helped create the Agricultural Adjustment Administration and championed experimental communities like Greenbelt, Maryland, before political opposition forced him out of the administration in 1936.

**Adolf Berle Jr.** (1932)
Adolf A. Berle Jr. was a Columbia University law professor and co-author of "The Modern Corporation and Private Property," a groundbreaking study of corporate power, who became one of the three original members of FDR's Brain Trust. He authored Roosevelt's famous 1932 Commonwealth Club Address outlining the case for government economic intervention and helped draft the Securities Act of 1933, which established federal regulation of the stock market for the first time.

**Frances Perkins** (1933)
Frances Perkins was appointed Secretary of Labor by FDR in 1933, making her the first woman ever to serve in a presidential cabinet — a position she held for all twelve years of Roosevelt's presidency. Widely considered the central architect of the New Deal's social programs, she championed the creation of Social Security, federal unemployment insurance, the minimum wage, the 40-hour workweek, and laws banning child labor.

**Harold L. Ickes** (1933)
Harold L. Ickes served as FDR's Secretary of the Interior from 1933 to 1946 and simultaneously directed the Public Works Administration (PWA), through which he oversaw billions of dollars in construction projects designed to create jobs and stimulate private investment during the Depression. Known as "Honest Harold" for his fierce opposition to corruption and waste, he was one of only two original Roosevelt cabinet members (along with Frances Perkins) to serve for the president's entire time in office.

**Hugh Johnson** (1933)
Hugh S. Johnson was a retired Army officer and Brain Trust member whom FDR appointed to lead the National Recovery Administration (NRA) in 1933, the ambitious New Deal agency that brought industry, labor, and government together to write "codes of fair competition" intended to raise wages and stabilize prices. He launched the iconic "Blue Eagle" publicity campaign to rally public support and was named Time magazine's Man of the Year for 1933, but his combative management style led to his forced resignation by October 1934.

**Henry Agard Wallace** (1933)
Henry A. Wallace, the son of a former Secretary of Agriculture, served as FDR's Secretary of Agriculture from 1933 to 1940 and later became his Vice President. He oversaw the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA), which paid farmers to reduce production in order to raise crop prices and rescue rural America from devastating poverty — a dramatic shift in federal agricultural policy that was central to the New Deal.

**Jesse Jones** (1933)
Jesse H. Jones was a Texas banker and entrepreneur whom FDR elevated to chairman of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) in 1933, transforming a Hoover-era agency into one of the most powerful instruments of the New Deal. Under Jones, the RFC's lending powers were vastly expanded to reopen failed banks, rescue railroads, and finance agriculture and housing — disbursing so much money that Jones was called "the fourth branch of government."

**Harry Hopkins** (1933)
Harry Hopkins was a social worker turned federal administrator who became FDR's most trusted relief official, running the Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA), the Civil Works Administration (CWA), and the Works Progress Administration (WPA) in rapid succession. The WPA alone employed 8.5 million people across 1.4 million projects — building schools, hospitals, airports, roads, and bridges — making Hopkins the driving force behind the New Deal's effort to replace cash handouts with paid work.

**John Collier** (1933)
John Collier was a social reformer and advocate for Native American rights whom FDR appointed as Commissioner of the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 1933. He engineered the "Indian New Deal," most notably the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, which reversed decades of forced cultural assimilation by ending the loss of reservation lands, enabling tribal self-government, and working to preserve Native American languages and traditions.

**Brandeis Group** (1933)
The Brandeis Group refers to the network of New Deal policymakers inspired by Supreme Court Justice Louis Brandeis's philosophy that concentrated economic power — what he called "the curse of bigness" — was a fundamental threat to democracy. His chief lieutenant, Harvard law professor Felix Frankfurter, placed dozens of young lawyers into the Roosevelt administration — most notably Thomas Corcoran and Benjamin Cohen, who drafted key reform legislation like the Securities Act and became increasingly influential during the Second New Deal as policy shifted from industrial planning toward trustbusting and financial regulation.

## Banking & Finance

**Glass-Steagall Act** (1933)
The Glass-Steagall Act, signed in June 1933, forced banks to choose between commercial banking (taking deposits and making loans) and investment banking (trading stocks and securities), so that ordinary people's savings could never again be gambled away on risky speculation. This separation was one of the most lasting New Deal reforms, helping to stabilize the American banking system for decades and restoring public trust after thousands of bank failures during the Great Depression.

**Federal Deposit Insurance** (1933)
Created by the Banking Act of 1933, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation guaranteed individual bank deposits up to $2,500 (later raised), meaning regular Americans would not lose their life savings if their bank went under. The FDIC was enormously effective at ending the epidemic of bank runs, since depositors no longer had reason to panic and rush to withdraw their money at the first sign of trouble.

**Emergency Banking Relief Act** (1933)
Passed on March 9, 1933 — just five days after FDR took office — the Emergency Banking Relief Act gave the president broad power over banking and allowed the Treasury Department to inspect and reopen only those banks that were financially sound. This was the legal backbone of the Bank Holiday, and it worked remarkably fast: when banks began reopening on March 13, deposits actually exceeded withdrawals, showing that public confidence had been restored almost overnight.

**Rehabilitate the Banks** (1933)
On March 6, 1933, President Roosevelt declared a nationwide "Bank Holiday," temporarily shutting down every bank in the country to stop the wave of bank failures and panicked withdrawals that had crippled the financial system. During the closure, federal examiners reviewed each bank's books, and only healthy banks were allowed to reopen, while FDR used his first "fireside chat" radio address to reassure Americans that their reopened banks were now safe.

**Go Off the Gold Standard** (1933)
In April 1933, FDR took the United States off the gold standard by issuing Executive Order 6102, which required citizens to turn in their gold coins and bullion and ended the policy of redeeming dollars for a fixed amount of gold. This gave the government freedom to expand the money supply and push prices upward, which was critical for helping debt-burdened farmers and businesses whose loans had become crushingly expensive during the deflation of the early 1930s.

**Gold Buying** (1933–1934)
Starting in October 1933, the Roosevelt administration began deliberately purchasing gold at increasingly higher prices as a way to drive down the value of the dollar and raise commodity prices, especially for struggling farmers. The program was controversial and somewhat chaotic — FDR and his advisors literally set gold prices over breakfast each morning — but it reflected the administration's urgent determination to fight deflation and stimulate economic recovery by any means available.

**Gold Reserve Act** (1934)
The Gold Reserve Act of January 1934 nationalized the country's gold supply by requiring all gold held by the Federal Reserve to be surrendered to the U.S. Treasury, and it officially devalued the dollar by raising the price of gold from $20.67 to $35 per ounce. This devaluation instantly boosted the Treasury's balance sheet by billions of dollars, gave the government greater control over monetary policy, and made American exports cheaper on the world market, helping to stimulate recovery.

**Securities Act** (1933)
The Securities Act of 1933, often called the "truth in securities" law, required companies selling stocks and bonds to the public to provide honest, detailed financial information and held them legally liable for misrepresentations. It was a direct response to the rampant fraud and deception that had fueled the speculative bubble of the late 1920s, and it established the principle that investors deserved access to accurate information before risking their money.

**Securities & Exchange Commission** (1934)
Created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, the SEC was a new federal agency charged with regulating the stock market, enforcing securities laws, and preventing the kind of manipulation and insider trading that had contributed to the 1929 crash. FDR appointed Joseph P. Kennedy — a man who knew Wall Street's tricks firsthand — as its first chairman, and the SEC became a permanent watchdog over American financial markets.

**Require Financial Statements** (1933–1934)
As part of the securities reforms of 1933–34, publicly traded companies were required for the first time to file regular, standardized financial statements that were available for public inspection. This transparency requirement was revolutionary because before the New Deal, companies could sell stock to the public while keeping their actual financial condition secret, leaving ordinary investors essentially gambling in the dark.

**Reconstruction Finance Corp.** (1932)
The Reconstruction Finance Corporation was actually created in January 1932 under President Hoover to make emergency loans to banks, railroads, and other large institutions that were on the verge of collapse during the Depression. Under Roosevelt, the RFC was dramatically expanded and became one of the most powerful agencies in Washington, eventually lending and investing billions of dollars across the economy — from bailing out banks to financing rural electrification.

**RFC Investments** (1933–1937)
Under FDR, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation went far beyond Hoover's original vision of emergency bank lending and began making direct equity investments in banks, funding public works, and financing everything from agricultural programs to exports. By the mid-1930s, the RFC had deployed billions of dollars throughout the economy, making it arguably the single most important financial tool of the New Deal and a model for how government could use strategic investment to fight an economic crisis.

**Fiscal Policy** (1933–1937)
New Deal fiscal policy represented a historic shift toward using government spending as a tool to fight economic downturns, an approach influenced by the ideas of economist John Maynard Keynes, who argued that when private spending collapsed, the government needed to step in and spend to fill the gap. While FDR never fully embraced deficit spending — he actually tried to balance the budget in 1937, triggering a sharp recession — the New Deal nonetheless established the precedent that the federal government bore responsibility for managing the overall health of the economy.

**Revenue Act** (1935)
The Revenue Act of 1935, nicknamed the "Wealth Tax Act" by the press, raised tax rates on the highest incomes, large corporations, and inherited wealth, reflecting FDR's argument that concentrated economic power was both unfair and dangerous to democracy. While critics like Senator Huey Long pushed Roosevelt toward this populist measure, the actual revenue it generated was modest — its real significance was political, signaling that the New Deal would challenge the extreme inequality of the era.

**Estate, Gift & Capital Taxes** (1935)
As part of the Revenue Act of 1935, Congress increased estate taxes on large inheritances, closed loopholes in gift taxes that the wealthy used to pass fortunes to their heirs tax-free, and raised taxes on capital gains. These provisions reflected the New Deal philosophy that extreme concentrations of inherited wealth undermined equal opportunity and democratic government, and they represented a direct challenge to the economic dynasties that had dominated the Gilded Age and the 1920s.

**Excess Profits Tax & Surtaxes** (1935)
The Revenue Act of 1935 imposed a graduated corporate income tax that hit larger corporations harder and added surtaxes on individual incomes above $50,000, with the top rate on incomes over $5 million reaching 75 percent. These provisions targeted what FDR called "an unjust concentration of wealth and economic power," aiming to prevent big corporations and the ultra-wealthy from capturing a disproportionate share of the nation's income while millions of Americans remained in poverty.

**Silver Purchase Act** (1934)
The Silver Purchase Act of 1934 directed the Treasury to buy large quantities of silver until it made up one-quarter of the nation's monetary reserves, effectively subsidizing Western silver-mining states and expanding the money supply. While politically popular with the powerful "Silver Bloc" of Western senators, the act had unintended consequences abroad — particularly destabilizing China's silver-based currency — and did relatively little to help the domestic recovery compared to other New Deal monetary policies.

**Public Utility Holding Co. Act** (1935)
The Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 targeted the massive, pyramid-like corporate structures that a handful of holding companies used to control most of the nation's electric and gas utilities, often at the expense of ordinary ratepayers. Its most controversial provision, the "death sentence" clause, gave the SEC power to break up any holding company that could not prove it served a useful economic purpose, and despite fierce opposition from the utility industry, the law eventually dismantled some of the most abusive corporate empires of the era.

**Anti-Trust Policy** (1937–1938)
New Deal antitrust policy went through a dramatic shift: initially, FDR's National Recovery Administration actually suspended antitrust laws to allow industries to cooperate and set prices, but after the Supreme Court struck down the NRA in 1935, the administration reversed course and launched an aggressive antitrust campaign led by Thurman Arnold at the Department of Justice. This later push to break up monopolies and promote competition reflected a genuine tension within the New Deal between those who wanted government-supervised cooperation among big businesses and those who believed concentrated corporate power itself was the root of the Depression.

## Industry & Labor

**National Industrial Recovery Act** (1933)
Signed into law by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 16, 1933, the NIRA was a sweeping piece of New Deal legislation that authorized the president to regulate industry by establishing codes of "fair competition," setting wages and prices, and creating the Public Works Administration. It was one of the most ambitious attempts to fight the Great Depression by getting business, labor, and government to cooperate, but the Supreme Court unanimously struck it down in May 1935, ruling it an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power.

**National Recovery Administration** (1933)
The NRA was the federal agency created under the NIRA and headed by General Hugh S. Johnson to oversee the drafting and enforcement of industry codes of fair competition. It approved 557 basic and 189 supplemental industry codes in just two years, making it one of the most far-reaching regulatory experiments in American history, though critics charged it with favoring big business and fostering monopoly-like behavior.

**NRA Codes** (1933–1935)
The NRA codes were detailed sets of rules negotiated between government officials and leaders of each industry that established minimum wages, maximum working hours, minimum prices for goods, and bans on child labor. These codes were meant to stop the destructive cycle of businesses undercutting each other on wages and prices during the Depression, but they were controversial because many small businesses felt the codes were written to benefit large corporations at their expense.

**We Do Our Part** (1933)
The Blue Eagle was a patriotic symbol — an eagle clutching a gear and lightning bolts — that businesses displayed in their windows and on products to show they were complying with NRA codes. President Roosevelt urged consumers to buy only from businesses displaying the Blue Eagle and its slogan "We Do Our Part," turning shopping into an act of national solidarity and pressuring non-compliant businesses with the threat of public boycott.

**Industry-Wide Agreements** (1933–1935)
Under the NRA, entire industries — from steel to textiles to coal mining — sat down together to negotiate binding codes of fair competition that applied to every company in that sector, covering wages, hours, prices, and trade practices. This approach was revolutionary because it replaced cutthroat competition with coordinated industry-wide planning, though critics warned it looked dangerously like government-sponsored cartels that crushed smaller competitors.

**Antitrust Law Exemptions** (1933–1935)
The NIRA temporarily suspended federal antitrust laws — including the Sherman Antitrust Act — so that competing businesses could legally cooperate to set prices, wages, and production levels without fear of prosecution. This was a dramatic reversal of decades of American economic policy, justified as an emergency measure to stop the deflationary spiral of the Depression, but it drew sharp criticism from those who believed it simply allowed big companies to fix prices and squeeze out competition.

**Collective Bargaining** (1933)
Section 7(a) of the NIRA guaranteed workers "the right to organize and bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing" and banned yellow-dog contracts that forced workers to promise not to join a union as a condition of employment. This provision triggered a massive wave of union organizing across the country, though its enforcement was weak and employers frequently found ways around it, which ultimately led Congress to pass the stronger Wagner Act in 1935.

**Worker's Rights** (1933–1935)
The New Deal represented the first time the federal government broadly sided with workers over employers, using legislation like the NIRA and the Wagner Act to guarantee rights to organize, set minimum labor standards, and outlaw exploitative practices. Before the New Deal, workers who tried to unionize could be legally fired, blacklisted, or met with violent strikebreaking, so these laws fundamentally shifted the balance of power between labor and management in the United States.

**Minimum Wages** (1933–1935)
The NRA codes established minimum wages ranging from 20 to 45 cents per hour depending on the industry, marking the first time the federal government set a floor on what American workers could be paid. Although these protections were lost when the Supreme Court struck down the NIRA in 1935, they laid the groundwork for the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, which created the first permanent federal minimum wage.

**Maximum Hours** (1933–1935)
The NRA codes capped the workweek at 35 to 45 hours depending on the industry, aiming both to improve working conditions and to spread available work among more people during a time of massive unemployment. By limiting how many hours each worker could put in, the Roosevelt administration hoped employers would be forced to hire additional workers, turning labor regulation into a job-creation strategy.

**Outlaw Child Labor** (1933–1935)
The NRA's "blanket code" effectively banned child labor across industries participating in the program, achieving something that reformers had been fighting for since the late 1800s and that two proposed constitutional amendments had failed to accomplish. The NRA all but wiped out child labor and sweatshops during its existence, and although the codes expired when the NIRA was struck down in 1935, permanent federal child labor restrictions were later enacted through the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.

**National Labor Relations Act** (1935)
Signed into law on July 5, 1935 and sponsored by Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York, the Wagner Act replaced the weak labor protections of the now-unconstitutional NIRA with a permanent, enforceable guarantee that private-sector workers could organize into unions, bargain collectively, and strike. It created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce these rights and is considered one of the most important pieces of labor legislation in American history, leading to a dramatic surge in union membership across the country.

**Sanction Unionization** (1935)
The Wagner Act made it federal policy to actively encourage and protect unionization, declaring that the "inequality of bargaining power" between employers and employees harmed the national economy and workers' well-being. Unlike the earlier NIRA, which merely stated a right to organize without effective enforcement, the Wagner Act gave the NLRB real power to hold elections, certify unions, and compel employers to bargain in good faith.

## Agriculture & Conservation

**Agricultural Adjustment Act** (1933)
The Agricultural Adjustment Act was a landmark federal law that paid farmers to reduce their crop production and livestock herds, aiming to raise farm prices that had collapsed during the Great Depression. While the program did help raise prices, it was controversial — critics were outraged that crops were plowed under and piglets slaughtered while many Americans went hungry, and the Supreme Court struck down the original AAA in 1936.

**Farm Subsidies** (1933–onward)
Farm subsidies were government payments to farmers introduced under the New Deal to keep agriculture profitable at a time when overproduction had driven crop prices so low that farming couldn't cover its costs. These payments fundamentally changed American agriculture by establishing the principle that the federal government had a responsibility to support farmers' incomes — a policy tradition that continues to this day through modern farm bills.

**Stabilize Crop Prices** (1933)
Stabilizing crop prices was the central goal of the Agricultural Adjustment Act, which used production controls and government purchasing programs to end the cycle of overproduction that had driven prices for wheat, cotton, corn, and other staples to historic lows. Without stable prices, farmers couldn't repay loans or feed their families, and widespread farm failures threatened to drag rural banks and entire regional economies down with them.

**Subsidize Scarcity** (1933)
"Subsidize scarcity" describes the AAA's controversial strategy of paying farmers to destroy existing crops and kill livestock in order to reduce surplus supply and drive prices back up. In 1933 alone, the government oversaw the plowing under of ten million acres of cotton and the slaughter of six million young pigs — actions that raised farm income by about 50 percent but sparked moral outrage given that millions of Americans were going hungry.

**Farm Bill** (1933–1938)
The New Deal era established the tradition of omnibus farm legislation, beginning with the AAA in 1933 and followed by the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936 and the second Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, which introduced the concept of an "ever-normal granary" to store surplus grain for lean years. These bills created the basic framework of American agricultural policy — including price supports, production controls, and crop insurance — that Congress has continued to reauthorize in recurring farm bills ever since.

**Soil Conservation Service** (1935)
The Soil Conservation Service was a federal agency established in 1935 under the Department of Agriculture in direct response to the catastrophic Dust Bowl, which saw massive dust storms strip topsoil from millions of acres of Great Plains farmland. The agency taught farmers sustainable practices like contour plowing, terracing, and crop rotation, helping to halt the environmental disaster and establishing soil conservation as a permanent federal responsibility.

**Tennessee Valley Authority** (1933)
The Tennessee Valley Authority was one of the most ambitious New Deal programs, creating a federal corporation to build dams, generate cheap hydroelectric power, control flooding, and promote economic development across the impoverished Tennessee River Valley spanning seven states. It brought electricity, jobs, and modern infrastructure to one of the poorest regions in America and remains a landmark example of large-scale government-led regional planning — though it was also controversial for displacing thousands of families whose land was flooded by new reservoirs.

**Rural Electrification Admin.** (1935)
The Rural Electrification Administration was created in 1935 to bring electric power to rural America, where only about 10 percent of farms had electricity compared to 90 percent of urban homes. By providing low-interest federal loans to rural electric cooperatives, the REA transformed daily life for millions of farm families — powering lights, radios, refrigerators, and water pumps — and is considered one of the most life-changing New Deal programs.

**Shelter Belt** (1934)
The Great Plains Shelterbelt was a massive federal project launched by Roosevelt in 1934 that planted over 200 million trees in long strips stretching from Texas to North Dakota, creating windbreaks to combat the devastating soil erosion of the Dust Bowl. These shelterbelts reduced wind speed across open farmland, held topsoil in place, and retained moisture, demonstrating that large-scale environmental restoration was possible.

**Canker, Moths & Weevil Control** (1930s)
During the New Deal era, federal agencies expanded programs to combat agricultural pests like citrus canker, gypsy moths, and the infamous boll weevil — a cotton-destroying beetle that had been devastating Southern agriculture since the early 1900s. These pest-control efforts put unemployed workers to a useful purpose while protecting the crop yields that New Deal price-stabilization programs were working to support.

**Civilian Conservation Corps** (1933)
The Civilian Conservation Corps was one of the first and most popular New Deal programs, employing about three million unmarried young men in outdoor conservation work such as planting trees, building trails, fighting forest fires, and controlling soil erosion. It provided jobs, food, shelter, and a small wage (most of which was sent home to workers' families) during the worst years of the Depression, while also planting over three billion trees and developing more than 800 state and national parks.

## Welfare & Public Works

**Social Security Act** (1935)
The Social Security Act, signed by President Roosevelt on August 14, 1935, created a federal system of old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and aid for dependent children and the disabled. It was one of the most lasting achievements of the New Deal, establishing for the first time a government safety net that promised Americans some basic economic security in old age and during hard times.

**FERA Program** (1933)
The Federal Emergency Relief Administration, created in May 1933 and led by Harry Hopkins, was one of the first major New Deal agencies, distributing $500 million in federal grants to state and local governments to run direct relief programs for the unemployed. FERA established the principle that the federal government — not just charities and local governments — had a responsibility to help citizens survive an economic catastrophe.

**Direct Relief** (1933–1935)
Direct relief referred to government cash payments or goods (like food and clothing) given straight to unemployed and destitute families during the Great Depression. While it kept millions of people alive, FDR and many New Dealers saw direct relief as demoralizing and preferred work relief programs that gave people jobs instead of handouts, which shaped how later programs like the WPA were designed.

**Civil Works Administration** (1933–1934)
The Civil Works Administration was a short-lived emergency jobs program created by Harry Hopkins in November 1933 that put over four million Americans to work within weeks building roads, schools, and parks. Although it was shut down by the spring of 1934 due to its high cost, the CWA proved that the federal government could quickly create mass employment and served as a model for the larger WPA that followed.

**Works Progress Administration** (1935)
The Works Progress Administration, launched in 1935 under Harry Hopkins, became the biggest New Deal jobs program, employing roughly 8.5 million Americans over its lifetime to build public buildings, roads, bridges, and parks, and to run arts, literacy, and youth programs. The WPA reflected FDR's belief that work relief was better than direct cash payments — it gave people paychecks and purpose while building infrastructure that communities still use today.

**Public Works Administration** (1933)
The Public Works Administration, created in 1933 under Secretary of the Interior Harold Ickes, funded large-scale construction projects like dams, bridges, hospitals, and schools by giving contracts to private construction firms. Unlike the WPA, which focused on employing as many people as possible, the PWA focused on big, lasting infrastructure — it was slower to spend money but produced major landmarks and stimulated heavy industry.

**Highways & Subways** (1933–1937)
New Deal agencies like the PWA and WPA invested heavily in transportation infrastructure, building or improving roughly 650,000 miles of roads, thousands of bridges, and urban transit systems including New York City subway extensions. These projects served a double purpose: they put unemployed workers on payrolls during the Depression and gave the country a modern transportation network that supported economic growth for decades afterward.

**Dams & Power Plants** (1933–1937)
The New Deal era saw the construction of massive dam and hydroelectric projects — including the completion of Hoover Dam (1936), the start of Grand Coulee Dam, and the dams of the Tennessee Valley Authority — which brought flood control, electricity, and jobs to some of America's poorest regions. These projects brought electric power to millions of rural Americans for the first time, transforming daily life and enabling economic development in areas that private utility companies had refused to serve.

**Hospitals & Housing Projects** (1933–1937)
Through agencies like the PWA, the New Deal funded the construction of hundreds of hospitals, public health clinics, and public housing developments across the country, including some of the first large-scale public housing projects in American cities. These projects addressed two crises at once: they put construction workers back on the job while tackling the terrible health and housing conditions that the Depression had made even worse.

**Schools & Playgrounds** (1933–1937)
New Deal agencies built or improved thousands of schools, playgrounds, parks, swimming pools, and community centers across America, with the WPA alone constructing roughly 5,900 new school buildings. These projects reflected the New Deal's belief that government investment in public spaces could strengthen local communities, improve children's lives, and create jobs all at the same time.

**US Housing Authority** (1937)
The United States Housing Authority, established by the Wagner-Steagall Housing Act of 1937, was a federal agency created to lend money to local governments so they could clear slums and build low-cost public housing. It represented the first time the federal government committed to an ongoing role in providing affordable housing for low-income Americans.

**Home Owners Loan Corp.** (1933)
The Home Owners' Loan Corporation, created in June 1933, refinanced roughly one million home mortgages that were in danger of foreclosure, rescuing both struggling homeowners and the banks that held their loans. While the HOLC saved countless families from losing their homes, it also introduced the practice of "redlining" — rating neighborhoods by perceived lending risk in ways that systematically disadvantaged Black communities, with effects that lasted for generations.

**Federal Housing Admin.** (1934)
The Federal Housing Administration, created by the National Housing Act of 1934, insured private mortgage loans so that banks would be willing to offer longer-term, lower-interest home loans to ordinary Americans. The FHA transformed homeownership in the United States by making it possible for middle-class families to buy houses with smaller down payments, though its lending guidelines also reinforced racial segregation by favoring white suburban neighborhoods.

**Resettlement Administration** (1935)
The Resettlement Administration, created by executive order in 1935 under Rexford Tugwell, aimed to help struggling small farmers by relocating families from worn-out land to planned communities and providing loans and technical assistance. Although controversial — critics called it socialistic — it helped thousands of farm families, and its photography division (employing artists like Dorothea Lange and Walker Evans) produced some of the most iconic images of the Depression era.

**Unemployment Insurance** (1935)
Unemployment insurance, established as part of the Social Security Act of 1935, created a joint federal-state system that provided temporary cash payments to workers who lost their jobs through no fault of their own. It was a revolutionary idea in the United States at the time — the concept that government should cushion workers against the shock of job loss — and it remains a core part of America's economic safety net today.

**Public Health Services** (1933–1937)
During the New Deal, federal spending on public health expanded significantly through agencies like the PWA and the Social Security Act's public health provisions, funding new hospitals, clinics, sanitation projects, and disease-prevention programs. This expansion mattered because millions of Americans — particularly in the South and in impoverished rural communities — had almost no access to modern healthcare before the federal government stepped in.

**Federal Art Project** (1935)
The Federal Art Project, a division of the WPA launched in 1935, employed thousands of out-of-work artists to create murals, sculptures, paintings, and posters for public buildings across the country. The federal government had never before supported the arts on such a scale — it kept artists fed during the Depression while filling post offices, schools, and libraries with artwork that brought culture into everyday American life.

**Federal Theatre** (1935–1939)
The Federal Theatre Project, a WPA program directed by Hallie Flanagan, employed actors, directors, and playwrights to stage free or low-cost plays for audiences across the country, including innovative productions that tackled social issues. It became one of the most controversial New Deal programs — Congress shut it down in 1939 amid accusations of communist influence — but it brought live theater to millions of Americans who had never seen a professional performance.

**Federal Writers Project** (1935)
The Federal Writers' Project, part of the WPA, employed thousands of out-of-work writers — including future literary figures like Ralph Ellison, Zora Neale Hurston, and Richard Wright — to produce state guidebooks, local histories, and oral history collections. Its most lasting contribution may be its slave narrative collection, in which interviewers recorded the life stories of formerly enslaved people, preserving firsthand accounts that became invaluable to historians.

**Federal Music Project** (1935)
The Federal Music Project, a WPA program launched in 1935, employed around 16,000 out-of-work musicians to perform free public concerts, teach music lessons, and collect traditional American folk songs. It brought live music — from classical orchestras to jazz ensembles — into communities that rarely had access to professional performances, while preserving folk music traditions that might otherwise have been lost.

**Black Cabinet** (1933–1945)
The Black Cabinet was an informal group of prominent African American advisors — including Mary McLeod Bethune, Robert Weaver, and others — who held positions in various New Deal agencies and met to advocate for Black Americans' inclusion in federal programs. While the group had no official power and the New Deal still largely accommodated segregation, the Black Cabinet marked the first time African Americans had meaningful access to the White House policy process, helping shift Black voters' allegiance from the Republican Party to the Democrats.

**National Youth Administration** (1935)
The National Youth Administration, created in 1935 as part of the WPA, provided part-time jobs and work-study programs to young Americans aged 16 to 25 so they could stay in school or gain work experience during the Depression. Led in part by Mary McLeod Bethune, who headed its Division of Negro Affairs, the NYA was notable for being one of the more racially inclusive New Deal programs, helping over 300,000 Black youth access education and employment opportunities.

**Bread Lines** (1930s)
Bread lines were long queues of hungry, unemployed people waiting to receive free food from charities, churches, or soup kitchens, and they became one of the most recognizable and devastating images of the Great Depression. The sight of thousands of Americans lining up just to eat helped build public support for the New Deal's relief programs by making it impossible to deny the scale of suffering caused by the economic collapse.

**Welfare State** (1933–1935)
The term "welfare state" refers to a system in which the government takes responsibility for protecting citizens' basic economic well-being through programs like social insurance, public jobs, and direct aid. The New Deal essentially built America's version of a welfare state — before the 1930s, the federal government played almost no role in helping ordinary people through hard times, but programs like Social Security and unemployment insurance fundamentally changed what Americans expected their government to do.

## Policies & Concepts

**Power of Government** (1933–1937)
During the Great Depression, President Roosevelt dramatically expanded the role of the federal government, creating dozens of new agencies and programs that put Washington in charge of things like banking regulation, job creation, and social insurance. This shift was controversial but marked a turning point in American history, establishing the idea that the federal government had a responsibility to protect citizens' economic well-being.

**Bold, Persistent Experimentation** (1932)
In a May 1932 commencement speech at Oglethorpe University, FDR declared that the country needed "bold, persistent experimentation," arguing that it was common sense to try something and, if it failed, to try something else. This philosophy became the guiding spirit of the New Deal, as Roosevelt's administration launched program after program — some successful, some not — in a pragmatic effort to combat the Depression rather than stick rigidly to any single ideology.

**Broad Programs** (1933–1938)
The New Deal encompassed an enormous range of legislation and executive actions, spanning everything from banking reform and farm aid to public works, electrification of rural areas, and new labor protections. No previous president had ever attempted to reshape so many parts of American life at once, and the sheer scope of these programs reflected how deeply the Great Depression had damaged nearly every sector of the economy.

**Social Welfare** (1933–1935)
Before the New Deal, the federal government offered almost no direct assistance to poor, elderly, or unemployed Americans — that was considered a job for charities and local governments. FDR's administration fundamentally changed this by creating programs like Social Security and federal relief agencies, establishing for the first time the principle that the national government had a duty to provide a basic safety net for its citizens.

**Unemployment Relief** (1933–1935)
With roughly one in four Americans out of work by 1933, the Roosevelt administration created a series of agencies — including the CCC, the CWA, and later the WPA — that directly hired millions of unemployed people for public projects. These programs not only provided paychecks to desperate families but also built roads, bridges, schools, and parks that communities used for decades afterward.

**Restore Faith of the Nation** (1933)
When FDR took office in March 1933, public confidence in the government and the economy had collapsed after more than three years of worsening depression under President Hoover. Roosevelt's energetic leadership style, optimistic tone, and rapid action during his first weeks in office helped convince millions of Americans that someone was finally taking charge, which was itself a critical ingredient in beginning economic recovery.

**Restore Shattered Economy** (1933–1937)
The U.S. economy had shrunk by roughly half between 1929 and 1933, with industrial production, farm prices, and wages all in freefall when Roosevelt entered office. The New Deal attacked this crisis on multiple fronts — stabilizing banks, raising agricultural prices, funding public works, and reforming financial markets — and while full recovery did not come until World War II, the economy grew significantly during FDR's first term.

**Federal Regulation** (1933–1935)
The New Deal created a wave of new federal regulatory agencies and laws, including the SEC to police Wall Street, the FDIC to protect bank depositors, and the NLRB to oversee labor relations. These regulations were designed to prevent the kind of reckless speculation and unchecked corporate power that many Americans blamed for causing the Depression in the first place.

**First 100 Days** (1933)
Between FDR's inauguration on March 4, 1933, and mid-June, Congress passed an unprecedented flood of fifteen major bills at the new president's urging, including the Emergency Banking Act, the Agricultural Adjustment Act, and the act creating the Tennessee Valley Authority. This extraordinary burst of legislative activity set the standard by which every subsequent president's early days in office have been measured.

**President's Speeches** (1933–1937)
FDR was one of the most gifted public communicators ever to hold the presidency, and he used speeches strategically to build support for New Deal programs, explain complex policies in plain language, and rally a demoralized nation. His addresses — whether delivered to Congress, at campaign events, or over the radio — were essential tools for pressuring reluctant lawmakers and maintaining the public enthusiasm he needed to push through his ambitious agenda.

**Fireside Chats** (1933–1944)
FDR's "Fireside Chats" were a series of roughly 30 evening radio addresses in which the president spoke directly to the American people in a calm, conversational tone, explaining his policies and asking for their support. The first chat, on March 12, 1933, reassured a panicked nation during the banking crisis and was so effective that deposits began flowing back into banks the very next day, demonstrating the enormous power of this new form of presidential communication.

**Issue Edicts** (1933–1937)
President Roosevelt made aggressive use of executive orders to implement New Deal policies, issuing more than 3,700 over his presidency — far more than any other president in history. Among the most significant was Executive Order 6102 in April 1933, which required citizens to turn in their gold coins and bullion to the Federal Reserve, a dramatic step that allowed the government to devalue the dollar and pursue an inflationary monetary policy.

**New Deal** (1933–1938)
The New Deal was the name given to the sweeping set of programs, regulations, and reforms that President Roosevelt launched to combat the Great Depression, a term he first used in his July 1932 speech accepting the Democratic presidential nomination. While historians debate how much the New Deal actually ended the Depression, it permanently transformed the relationship between the American people and their government, creating institutions like Social Security and the SEC that remain central to American life today.

**Propose Bills** (1933–1937)
FDR sent an extraordinary number of legislative proposals to Congress, fundamentally shifting the balance of power between the executive and legislative branches by making the president, rather than Congress, the primary driver of the nation's lawmaking agenda. During the famous First 100 Days alone, Roosevelt proposed and Congress passed fifteen major pieces of legislation, a pace of executive-led lawmaking that was virtually without precedent in American history.

**Sign Bills into Law** (1933–1937)
During his first term, Roosevelt signed an enormous volume of landmark legislation, including the Social Security Act, the National Industrial Recovery Act, the Wagner Act, and the Banking Acts of 1933 and 1935, among many others. This prolific bill-signing reflected both the severity of the Depression crisis and FDR's ability to work with large Democratic majorities in Congress, producing a lasting transformation of American law and government.

**Repeal Prohibition** (1933)
The Twenty-First Amendment, ratified on December 5, 1933, repealed the Eighteenth Amendment and ended the nationwide ban on alcoholic beverages that had been in effect since 1920. FDR had campaigned on repeal, and ending Prohibition was both enormously popular with the public and economically beneficial, as it created jobs, generated new tax revenue, and eliminated a major source of organized crime.

**Relief Recovery Reform** (1933–1938)
The "Three R's" — Relief, Recovery, and Reform — described the three main goals of FDR's New Deal: providing immediate relief to the suffering, promoting economic recovery from the Depression, and enacting long-term reforms to prevent future crises. This framework helped organize and explain an enormous and sometimes confusing array of programs, from emergency food assistance (relief) to public works spending (recovery) to the creation of the SEC and Social Security (reform).

**Ratify Amendment** (1933)
Two constitutional amendments were ratified in 1933 alone: the Twentieth Amendment (ratified January 23), which moved Inauguration Day from March 4 to January 20 to shorten the "lame duck" period, and the Twenty-First Amendment (ratified December 5), which repealed Prohibition. The Twentieth Amendment was particularly timely because the long gap between FDR's election in November 1932 and his inauguration in March 1933 had left the country drifting without effective leadership during the worst months of the banking crisis.

**Century of Progress** (1933–1934)
The Century of Progress International Exposition was a World's Fair held in Chicago from 1933 to 1934, celebrating a hundred years of technological and scientific advancement since the city's founding. Despite opening during the worst of the Great Depression, the fair attracted nearly 49 million visitors and actually turned a profit, offering Americans a welcome dose of optimism and a futuristic vision at a time when the present seemed bleak.

**Far Reaching Legislation** (1933–1938)
The laws passed during the New Deal were remarkable not just in number but in scope, reaching into areas of American life — labor relations, agriculture, banking, housing, social insurance, electrical power — that the federal government had largely left alone before the Depression. Landmark statutes like the Social Security Act and the Wagner Act created entirely new frameworks for how Americans worked, retired, and related to their government, many of which remain in effect today.

**Stunning Press Release** (1933–1937)
FDR's administration revolutionized presidential communication with the press, holding frequent and informal press conferences — roughly two per week — that gave Roosevelt an unmatched ability to shape news coverage and public opinion. The president's press secretary, Stephen Early, also pioneered the strategic use of press releases and media access to build support for New Deal programs, making the Roosevelt White House far more media-savvy than any of its predecessors.

**Inflationary Proposal** (1933–1934)
Some of FDR's advisors pushed for deliberately inflationary policies such as devaluing the dollar, expanding the money supply, and abandoning the gold standard, arguing that falling prices were deepening the Depression. These proposals were controversial — conservatives and bankers warned they would destroy the currency — but Roosevelt adopted several of them, most notably taking the United States off the gold standard in 1933 and devaluing the dollar against gold in January 1934.

**Controlled Inflation** (1933–1934)
As part of his recovery strategy, FDR pursued a policy of managed inflation to reverse the devastating deflation of 1929–1933, during which falling prices had crushed farmers, increased the real burden of debt, and discouraged business investment. By taking the country off the gold standard, devaluing the dollar, and expanding credit through the Federal Reserve, the administration succeeded in gradually raising prices, which helped ease the debt burden on millions of farmers, homeowners, and businesses.

**Second Hundred Days** (1935)
The "Second Hundred Days" refers to the burst of major legislation Congress passed in the summer of 1935, including the Social Security Act, the Wagner Act, the Works Progress Administration, and the Wealth Tax Act. This second wave of New Deal legislation was in many ways more far-reaching and permanent than the first, as it created the modern American social safety net and established labor rights that fundamentally reshaped the relationship between workers, employers, and the government.

**Deep Rooted Reform** (1935–1938)
As the immediate economic emergency of 1933 eased, the New Deal increasingly shifted its focus from temporary relief measures to deep structural reforms aimed at preventing future depressions and reducing inequality. Programs like Social Security, the SEC, and the FDIC were designed not just to fix the current crisis but to permanently change how the American economy and government operated, embedding safeguards that would protect ordinary citizens for generations.

**Economic Legislation** (1933–1938)
The New Deal produced a torrent of economic legislation that transformed the American financial system, including the Glass-Steagall Act, the Securities Exchange Act, and the Banking Act of 1935. Together, these laws imposed a new framework of rules and oversight on Wall Street and the banking industry, creating a more stable financial system that helped prevent another crash of the 1929 variety for decades.

**Sense of Purpose** (1933–1937)
One of FDR's greatest achievements was restoring a sense of national purpose and morale to a country that had been demoralized by years of economic collapse, breadlines, and political paralysis. Through his energetic leadership, optimistic rhetoric, and the visible results of programs like the CCC and WPA — which put millions of Americans back to work building things they could see and use — Roosevelt convinced many citizens that the crisis could be overcome through collective action.

## Crisis Cards

**Outbursts of Violence** (1930s)
As the Depression deepened and millions of Americans faced hunger, eviction, and unemployment, episodes of violence erupted across the country, including clashes between strikers and police, farmers forcibly blocking foreclosure auctions, and riots by desperate workers. These outbursts reflected the genuine fear that if the government could not deliver relief, the social order itself might collapse.

**Protests Against Foreclosures** (1932–1933)
As farm prices collapsed during the Depression, thousands of farmers faced foreclosure on their land, prompting organized resistance across the Midwest, where groups of farmers physically blocked foreclosure sales, intimidated bidders, and sometimes threatened judges. These "penny auctions" — where neighbors bid absurdly low prices to return seized property to its owner — helped push the federal government to create agencies like the Farm Credit Administration to refinance agricultural debt.

**Bank Closures** (1930–1933)
Between 1930 and 1933, approximately 9,000 American banks failed, wiping out the savings of millions of depositors who had no federal insurance to protect them. By the time FDR took office in March 1933, the banking system had essentially ceased to function, with governors in dozens of states having already declared "bank holidays" to prevent further collapse.

**Fear Itself** (1933)
In his first inaugural address on March 4, 1933, FDR declared that "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself — nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror," directly addressing the paralyzing panic that had gripped the nation as banks collapsed and unemployment soared. The speech is considered one of the most important in American history because it reframed the Depression as a challenge that could be overcome through courage and action, setting the tone for the aggressive program of government intervention that followed.

**Farm Crisis** (1929–1933)
American farmers had already been struggling throughout the 1920s, but the Depression made things catastrophic: crop prices fell so low that corn was cheaper to burn than coal, milk was dumped in ditches because it cost more to transport than it was worth, and hundreds of thousands of farm families lost their land. The New Deal responded with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, which paid farmers to reduce production in order to raise prices — a radical approach that provided desperately needed income to rural America.

**Wall Street Opposition** (1934–1937)
Many bankers, stockbrokers, and financiers on Wall Street fiercely opposed the New Deal, viewing regulations like the Securities Exchange Act and Glass-Steagall as dangerous government overreach that would strangle free enterprise. Organizations like the American Liberty League mounted a well-funded campaign against Roosevelt's policies, though FDR effectively turned their opposition into a political asset by casting himself as the champion of ordinary Americans against the "economic royalists."

**Poverty** (1929–1939)
The Great Depression plunged millions of Americans into desperate poverty, with families losing their homes, surviving on bread and soup from charity kitchens, and children going without adequate food, clothing, or medical care. At the Depression's worst point, an estimated 60–80 million Americans — roughly half the population — were living in poverty, a scale of suffering that fundamentally changed how the country thought about the government's responsibility to its poorest citizens.

**Run on Banks** (1930–1933)
A bank run occurs when panicked depositors rush to withdraw their money all at once, and during the early 1930s, waves of bank runs swept across the country as people lost confidence that their savings were safe. Because banks had lent out most of their deposits, they could not pay everyone at once, causing even healthy banks to collapse — a chain reaction that led directly to the creation of the FDIC in 1933, which insured deposits and largely ended the phenomenon.

**Unemployment** (1929–1939)
Unemployment during the Great Depression reached staggering levels, rising from about 3% in 1929 to approximately 25% by 1933 — meaning roughly 13 million Americans who wanted to work could not find jobs. This mass unemployment was the defining human crisis of the Depression, driving the creation of New Deal work programs like the CCC and WPA, which at their peak employed millions of people on public projects.

**Lost Foreign Trade** (1930–1933)
The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, signed by President Hoover, raised tariffs on thousands of imported goods to record levels, prompting retaliatory tariffs from trading partners and contributing to a collapse of international trade — American exports fell from about $5.2 billion in 1929 to $1.7 billion by 1933. The New Deal addressed this through the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, which gave the president authority to negotiate lower tariffs with individual countries.

**Stock Market Crash** (1929)
The stock market crash of October 1929, with its catastrophic "Black Tuesday" on October 29, wiped out millions of investors and is widely seen as the event that triggered the Great Depression, though underlying economic weaknesses had been building throughout the 1920s. The crash exposed reckless speculation, inadequate regulation, and dangerous practices like buying stocks on margin with borrowed money — all of which the New Deal later addressed through the creation of the SEC.

**Homelessness** (1929–1941)
As the Depression threw millions out of work and out of their homes, makeshift shantytowns known as "Hoovervilles" — sarcastically named after President Hoover — sprang up in cities across America, with families living in shelters cobbled together from scrap wood, cardboard, and tin. These encampments became powerful symbols of the government's failure to address the crisis and helped build public support for the New Deal's housing and relief programs.

**Dust Bowl** (1931–1939)
The Dust Bowl was an ecological catastrophe caused by severe drought and decades of poor farming practices on the Great Plains, which turned millions of acres of topsoil into massive dust storms that blackened skies from Texas to the Dakotas and beyond. The disaster displaced hundreds of thousands of people — the "Okies" and other migrants who fled to California — and prompted New Deal agencies like the Soil Conservation Service to promote better land management.

**Labor Disputes** (1933–1937)
The 1930s saw an explosion of labor conflict, including massive strikes in the auto, steel, textile, and longshoremen industries, as workers fought for union recognition, better wages, and improved conditions. The New Deal both fueled and attempted to channel this unrest through the Wagner Act of 1935, which guaranteed workers the right to organize and bargain collectively, leading to a dramatic expansion of union membership.

**Political Challenges** (1933–1937)
FDR faced opposition from all directions: conservatives and business leaders accused him of socialism, while populists like Huey Long and Father Charles Coughlin criticized the New Deal as too timid and demanded more radical wealth redistribution. These pressures actually helped shape the New Deal's evolution, as Roosevelt pushed through the more ambitious "Second New Deal" legislation in 1935 partly to outflank populist challengers.

**Riots** (1930s)
Hunger riots, eviction riots, and clashes between unemployed workers and police broke out in American cities throughout the early 1930s, including a deadly confrontation at Ford's River Rouge plant in 1932 where security forces killed four marchers. These episodes of civil unrest — along with the 1932 Bonus Army incident, in which U.S. troops forcibly dispersed World War I veterans camped in Washington — underscored the real danger that economic desperation could lead to broader social upheaval.

**Unfair Labor Practices** (1935)
Section 8 of the Wagner Act defined specific employer actions as illegal "unfair labor practices," including interfering with workers' right to organize, dominating company unions, discriminating against union members, and refusing to bargain collectively. This was groundbreaking because for the first time the federal government clearly spelled out what employers could not do to workers who wanted to unionize, and gave the NLRB the authority to investigate complaints and take violators to court.

**Opposition by Big Business** (1934–1936)
Many of America's most powerful corporate leaders bitterly opposed the New Deal, especially after the passage of the Wagner Act and Social Security in 1935, viewing these programs as attacks on free enterprise and steps toward socialism. The American Liberty League, backed by executives from DuPont, General Motors, and other major corporations, spent heavily to defeat Roosevelt in the 1936 election, but FDR won in a historic landslide carrying every state except Maine and Vermont.

**Eastern Floods** (1936)
In March 1936, massive floods devastated the northeastern United States, particularly along rivers in New England and the mid-Atlantic states, killing over 150 people, leaving hundreds of thousands homeless, and causing hundreds of millions of dollars in damage. The disaster reinforced the New Deal's argument for large-scale federal infrastructure projects like dams and flood control systems, and agencies like the WPA played major roles in both emergency relief and rebuilding.

**Supreme Court Rulings** (1935–1936)
In 1935 and 1936, the Supreme Court struck down several key New Deal programs as unconstitutional, including the National Industrial Recovery Act and the Agricultural Adjustment Act, ruling that Congress had overstepped its powers. These rulings provoked a major constitutional crisis and led FDR to propose his controversial "court-packing" plan in 1937, which failed in Congress but may have influenced the Court's subsequent shift toward upholding New Deal legislation.

**Recession** (1937–1938)
Just as the economy seemed to be recovering, a sharp recession hit in the fall of 1937, with industrial production plunging 33% and unemployment jumping back to roughly 19%, largely because the Roosevelt administration had prematurely cut government spending in an effort to balance the budget. The "Roosevelt Recession" emboldened New Deal critics but also seemed to validate economist John Maynard Keynes's argument that government spending was essential to maintain economic recovery — a lesson that influenced federal economic policy for decades.
